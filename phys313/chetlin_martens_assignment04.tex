\begin{problem}{1}
A system in thermal contact with a reservoir at temperature \( \tau \) has states \( s \) with energies \( \varepsilon_s \).
The probability of finding the system in state \( s \) is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:p1-state-probability}
  P(\varepsilon_s) = \frac{e^{-\varepsilon_s/\tau}}{Z},
  \qquad \text{where} \qquad Z = \sum_s e^{-\varepsilon_s/\tau} = e^{-F/\tau}.
\end{equation}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item Show that the entropy of the system can be written as
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:p1-gibbs-entropy}
    \sigma = -\sum_s P(\varepsilon_s)\log P(\varepsilon_s).
  \end{equation}
  [Hint: Start with the expression for \( \log P(\varepsilon_s) \).]

  This expression for the entropy is useful when the probabilities of the system being in each of its states are known but the partition function is not.
        \begin{proof}
    From the definition of \( P(\varepsilon_{s}) \),
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:p1-log-probability}
        \begin{aligned}
            \log P(\varepsilon_s)
            &= \log\left(\frac{e^{-\varepsilon_s/\tau}}{Z}\right)\\
            &= -\frac{\varepsilon_s}{\tau} - \log Z = \frac{F-\varepsilon_s}{\tau},
        \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}
    where we have used \( \log Z = -F/\tau \).

    Multiplying by \( -P(\varepsilon_s) \) and summing over all states,
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:p1-entropy-sum}
        \begin{aligned}
            -\sum_s P(\varepsilon_s)\log P(\varepsilon_s)
            &= -\sum_s P(\varepsilon_s)\frac{F-\varepsilon_s}{\tau}\\
            &= \frac{1}{\tau}\sum_s P(\varepsilon_s)\varepsilon_s
            - \frac{F}{\tau}\sum_s P(\varepsilon_s)\\
            &= \frac{U-F}{\tau},
        \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}
    where we have used the normalization condition
    \( \sum_s P(\varepsilon_s)=1 \) and the definition of the
    average internal energy
    \( U=\sum_s P(\varepsilon_s)\varepsilon_s \).

    From the definition of free energy,
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:p1-helmholtz}
        F=U-\tau\sigma,
    \end{equation}
    we obtain
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:p1-gibbs-entropy-result}
        \boxed{
            \sigma=\frac{U-F}{\tau}
            =-\sum_s P(\varepsilon_s)\log P(\varepsilon_s)
        }.
    \end{equation}
        \end{proof}
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}

The ideal gas (for Problems 2 and 3).
We did not cover the ideal gas in class.
A summary of its properties is given below.
For a monatomic gas of \( N \) particles of mass \( M \) in a volume \( V \) at temperature \( \tau \):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \( U = \frac{3}{2}N\tau \)
  \item \( pV = N\tau \)
  \item \( \sigma = N\bigl[\log(n_Q/n) + \frac{5}{2}\bigr] \), where \( n = N/V \) and \( n_Q = (M\tau/2\pi\hbar^2)^{3/2} \)
\end{itemize}
Also pay attention to the change in the entropy of the system for a reversible process.
If an isolated system undergoes a reversible process, its entropy \( \sigma_S \) remains constant.
If a system in contact with a thermal reservoir undergoes a reversible process, the total entropy \( \sigma_S + \sigma_R \) does not change, but the entropy of the system can change, with \( \Delta\sigma_S + \Delta\sigma_R = 0 \).

\begin{problem}{2}
A monatomic ideal gas is in thermal equilibrium with a reservoir at temperature \( \tau \).
The gas expands reversibly, its volume increasing by a small amount \( \delta V \), and it does work \( \delta W \) on its surroundings.
Find the change in the energy of the gas \( \delta U \), the heat \( \delta Q \) flowing into the gas, and the change in its free energy \( \delta F \).
Express each in terms of \( \delta W \).
\end{problem}
\begin{answer}
  The energy of an ideal gas is \( U = \frac{3}{2}N\tau \), that is, it entirely depends on the temperature; since \(\tau\) is constant, \(\Delta U = 0\).
  Moreover, since \( \Delta U = \Delta Q - \Delta W = 0 \), we have \(\Delta Q = \Delta W\).
  The free energy is \( F = U - \tau\sigma \), so that at constant temperature, \( \Delta F = \Delta U - \tau \Delta \sigma = -\tau \Delta \sigma\).

  \(\Delta \sigma + \Delta \sigma_R = 0\), and \(\Delta \sigma_R = -\Delta \frac{Q}{\tau} \), meaning that \( \Delta \sigma = \Delta \frac{Q}{\tau} \), so we get \(\Delta F = -\tau(\Delta \frac{Q}{\tau}) = -\Delta W\).
\end{answer}

\begin{problem}{3}
The same gas is now thermally isolated.
It again expands reversibly, its volume increasing by \( \delta V \), and it does work \( \delta W \) on its surroundings.
Find \( \delta U \), \( \delta Q \), the temperature change \( \delta\tau \), and \( \delta F \).
Express each in terms of \( \delta W \).
\end{problem}
\begin{answer}
  Thermal isolation means \(\Delta Q = 0\).
  Reversible means \(\Delta \sigma = 0\).
  We can find the change in energy since \( \Delta U = \Delta Q - \Delta W = -\Delta W \).
  For \(\Delta \tau\), we know \( U = \frac{3}{2}N \tau \) for an ideal gas, so at constant \( N \), we have \( \Delta\tau = \frac{2}{3N}\Delta U = \frac{-2}{3N}\Delta W \).

  Finally, since \( F = U - \tau\sigma \), \(\Delta F = \Delta U - \sigma \Delta \tau = -\Delta W - \frac{2}{3N}\Delta W \sigma = -\Delta W(1 - \frac{2\sigma}{3N})\).
\end{answer}

\begin{problem}{4}
Treat the Sun and the Earth as perfect blackbodies.
Use:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:p4-sun-earth-data}
  \begin{aligned}
    \text{Surface temperature of the Sun} \quad & T_{\odot} = 5800\,\mathrm{K} \\
    \text{Radius of the Sun} \quad & R_{\odot} = 6.96\times 10^8\,\mathrm{m} \\
    \text{Radius of the Earth} \quad & R_E = 6.37\times 10^6\,\mathrm{m} \\
    \text{Earth--Sun distance} \quad & d = 1.50\times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{m}
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The Earth rotates fast enough that its surface temperature \( T_E \) may be taken as uniform.
Estimate \( T_E \).
\end{problem}
\begin{answer}
  By definition, the radiant energy flux density for the sun is \( J_{\odot} = \sigma_B T_{\odot}^4 \) with \(\sigma_B = \pi^2 k_B^4 / 60 \hbar^3 c^3 \), so that the power emitted by the sun is \( T_{\odot} = A_{\odot}J_{\odot} = 4\pi R_{\odot}^2\sigma_BT_{\odot}^4\).

  Since this power is diffused radially out, at the surface of the Earth, the flux density from the Sun is \( J_{\oplus} = \frac{P_{\odot}}{4\pi d^2} \).

  Now, the radiation only reaches one hemisphere of the Earth, and in particular the flux is only proportional to the area which is normal to the incoming flux, that is, if \(\Sigma\) is one hemisphere of the surface of the Earth
  \begin{equation}
  \label{eq:6}
  \int\limits_{\Sigma} \cos\theta dA = \cos\theta R^2_{\oplus} \sin\theta \dd \theta \dd \phi = \pi R_{\oplus}^2,
\end{equation}
which means that the power at the surface of the Earth is \( P_{\text{in}} = \pi R_{\oplus}^2 J_{\oplus} = \frac{\pi R_{\oplus}^2 4\pi R_{\odot}^2\sigma_BT^4}{4\pi d^2} = \frac{\pi R_{\oplus}^2 R_{\odot}^2 \sigma_{B} T^{4}}{d^2} \).

Then, the power radiated out by the Earth is, just like the Sun, \( P_{\text{out}} = 4\pi R_{\oplus}^2\sigma_BT_{\oplus}^2 \).
Equilibrium occurs when
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    P_{\text{in}} &= P_{\text{out}} = \frac{\pi R_{\oplus}^2 R_{\odot}^2 \sigma_B T_{\odot}^4}{d^2} = 4\pi R_{\oplus}^2 \sigma_{B} T_{\oplus}^{4} \\
    \Rightarrow &\frac{R_{\odot}^2 T_{\odot}^4}{d^2} = 4T_{\oplus}^{4},
  \end{aligned}
\label{eq:7}
\end{equation}
showing that \( T_{\oplus}^{4} = \frac{1}{4} \frac{R_{\odot}^2 T _{\odot}^4}{d^2} = 6.0 \times 10^{9} \), or
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:8}
T_{\odot} = 2.8 \times 10^2 \text{K} \approx 280 \text{K}.
\end{equation}
\end{answer}

\begin{problem}{5}
A cavity of volume \( V \) is in thermal contact with a reservoir at temperature \( \tau \).
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item Show that the number of thermal photons in the cavity is
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:p5-photon-number}
    N = \frac{2\zeta(3)}{\pi^2}V\left(\frac{\tau}{\hbar c}\right)^3
    \approx 0.244\,V\left(\frac{\tau}{\hbar c}\right)^3,
  \end{equation}
  where \( \zeta(3) = 1.202 \).
  You may use \( \displaystyle\int_0^{\infty}\frac{x^2\,dx}{e^x-1} = 2\zeta(3) \).
        \begin{proof}
          We saw in class that the expected number of photons in a mode with frequency \(\omega_n\) is
          \begin{equation}
          \label{eq:3}
          \langle \mathcal{S}_n \rangle = \frac{1}{\exp(\hbar \omega_n/\tau) - 1},
        \end{equation}
        so that the total number is \( N = \sum\limits_n \mathcal{S}_n \) which we can approximate for large \( n \) as
        \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:4}
        n = \int\limits_{0}^{\infty} \mathcal{S}_n dN
        \end{equation}
        where \( n^2 = n_x^2 + n_y^2 + n_z^2 \).
        Using the allowed energies of a photon \( E_n = \frac{\pi\hbar c}{L}n \), and the distribution of triples \( dN = \frac{\pi}{2} n^2 \dd{n} \), we have
        \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:9}
        \begin{aligned}
        N &= \pi \int\limits_0^{\infty}  \frac{\dd{n}n^2}{\exp(\pi\hbar c n/L\tau) - 1}.
        \end{aligned}
      \end{equation}
      Making the substitution \( x = \frac{\pi\hbar c}{L\tau}n \), \( \dd{x} = \frac{\pi\hbar c}{L\tau} \dd{n} \), we have
      \begin{equation}
      \label{eq:10}
      n^2 \dd{n} = \left( \frac{L\tau}{\pi\hbar c} \right)^2 x^2 \frac{L\tau}{\pi\hbar c} \dd{x},
    \end{equation}
    and hence
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:11}
    \begin{aligned}
      N &= \pi \left( \frac{L\tau}{\pi\hbar c} \right)^3 \int\limits_{0}^{\infty} \frac{x^2 \dd{x}}{\exp(x) - 1} = \left( \frac{L\tau}{\hbar c} \right)^3 \frac{1}{\pi^2} 2\zeta(3) \\
      &= \frac{2\zeta(3)}{\pi^2} V \left( \frac{\tau}{\hbar c} \right)^3,
    \end{aligned}
  \end{equation}
  as desired.
        \end{proof}
  \item The cosmic microwave background is blackbody radiation at \( T = 2.725\,\mathrm{K} \).
  Estimate the number of CMB photons per cubic centimeter.
        \begin{answer}
    From part (a), the number density of thermal photons is
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:p5-cmb-density}
        \frac{N}{V}
        = \frac{2\zeta(3)}{\pi^2}
        \left(\frac{\tau}{\hbar c}\right)^3
        \approx 0.244\left(\frac{k_B T}{\hbar c}\right)^3,
    \end{equation}
    where we have used \( \tau = k_B T \).

    For the cosmic microwave background, \( T = 2.725\,\mathrm{K} \).
    Using
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:p5-constants}
        \begin{aligned}
            k_B &= 1.380649 \times 10^{-23}\,\mathrm{J/K},\\
            \hbar &= 1.054571817 \times 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s},\\
            c &= 2.99792458 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m/s},
        \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}
    we substitute into \cref{eq:p5-cmb-density} to obtain
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:p5-cmb-result}
        \begin{aligned}
            \frac{N}{V}
            &\approx 0.244
            \left(
                \frac{
                    (1.380649 \times 10^{-23})(2.725)
                }{
                    (1.054571817 \times 10^{-34})
                    (2.99792458 \times 10^8)
                }
            \right)^3\\
            &\approx 4.11 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\\
            &\approx \boxed{411\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}}.
        \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}

    Thus, there are approximately \( 411 \) CMB photons per cubic
    centimeter of space.
        \end{answer}

  \item Using the Stefan--Boltzmann result \( U/V = \pi^2\tau^4/15\hbar^3c^3 \), find the average energy per photon in units of \( \tau \).
        \begin{answer}
 The average energy per photon is the total energy of the radiation
    divided by the number of photons,
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:p5-average-energy}
        \langle E \rangle = \frac{U}{N} = \frac{U/V}{N/V}.
    \end{equation}

    From the Stefan--Boltzmann law, the energy density of blackbody
    radiation is
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:p5-energy-density}
        u = \frac{U}{V}
        = \frac{\pi^2}{15}\frac{\tau^4}{\hbar^3 c^3}.
    \end{equation}

    From part (a), the photon number density is
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:p5-number-density}
        n = \frac{N}{V}
        = \frac{2\zeta(3)}{\pi^2}
        \frac{\tau^3}{\hbar^3 c^3}.
    \end{equation}

    Substituting into \cref{eq:p5-average-energy}, we obtain
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:p5-average-energy-result}
        \begin{aligned}
            \langle E \rangle
            &= \frac{u}{n} = \frac{
                \dfrac{\pi^2}{15}\dfrac{\tau^4}{\hbar^3 c^3}
            }{
                \dfrac{2\zeta(3)}{\pi^2}
                \dfrac{\tau^3}{\hbar^3 c^3}
            }\\
            &= \frac{\pi^4}{30\zeta(3)}\tau \approx \boxed{2.701\,\tau}.
        \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}

    Thus, the average energy per thermal photon is approximately
    \( 2.701 \) times the thermal energy scale \( \tau = k_B T \).
        \end{answer}
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}{6}
A cubical cavity of volume \( V = L^3 \) contains thermal radiation.
The mode frequencies are
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:p6-mode-frequencies}
  \omega_j = \frac{\pi c}{L}\sqrt{n_x^2+n_y^2+n_z^2},
\end{equation}
where \( j \) labels the modes.
A microstate of the radiation is specified by the number of photons \( s_j \) in each mode, and the total energy in the cavity is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:p6-cavity-energy}
  U = \sum_j s_j\hbar\omega_j.
\end{equation}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item The pressure is \( p = -(\partial U/\partial V)_{\sigma} \).
  If the volume is changed slowly enough, the occupation numbers \( s_j \) do not change, so the entropy stays constant.
  Use this to write the pressure of the photon gas as a sum over modes.
        \begin{answer}
          We differentiate, holding \( s_j \) fixed,
          \begin{equation}
          \label{eq:12}
          p = - \pdv{U}{V} = \sum\limits_j-\pdv{V}(s_j\hbar\omega_{j}) = -\hbar \sum\limits_j s_j \pdv{\omega_j}{V}.
          \end{equation}
        \end{answer}

  \item Show that
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:p6-frequency-volume-derivative}
    \frac{d\omega_j}{dV} = -\frac{\omega_j}{3V}.
  \end{equation}
        \begin{proof}
          Each mode is \(\omega_j = \frac{\pi c}{L}\sqrt{n_{x}^2 + n_y^2 + n_z^2}\), and since \( L = V^{1/3} \), we have, writing \( n^2 = n_x^2 + n_y^2 + n_z^2 \),
          \begin{equation}
          \label{eq:13}
          \begin{aligned}
            \pdv{\omega_j}{V} &= \left( \pi c n \right) (- \frac{1}{3}V^{-4/3}) \\
            &= \frac{\pi c n}{V^{1/3}} \left(\frac{-1}{3V}\right) = -\frac{\omega_j}{3V}.
          \end{aligned}
          \end{equation}
        \end{proof}

  \item Show that the pressure of thermal radiation is
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:p6-radiation-pressure}
    p = \frac{U}{3V}.
  \end{equation}
        \begin{proof}
          Substituting \cref{eq:13} into \cref{eq:12} and recalling \cref{eq:p6-cavity-energy},
          \begin{equation}
          \label{eq:14}
          p = -\hbar \sum\limits_{j} s_j \pdv{\omega_j}{V} = -\hbar \sum\limits_j s_j \frac{-\omega_j}{3V} = \frac{1}{3V} \sum\limits_{j} s_{j}\hbar\omega_{j} = \frac{U}{3V}.
          \end{equation}
        \end{proof}
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}{7}
Two large parallel black plates are held at temperatures \( T_1 \) (upper) and \( T_2 \) (lower), with \( T_1 > T_2 \).
The gap between them is evacuated, so energy crosses only by radiation.

See figure 1 in the original problem set (the unnumbered diagram in Problem 7).
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item Find the net energy flux density \( J \) (power per unit area) from the upper plate to the lower one.
        \begin{answer}
          The flux density from the upper plate is \( J_{1} = \sigma_BT_{1}^4 \) and from the lower plate \( J_2 = \sigma_BT_2^4 \), so that the net flux out of the lower plate is
          \begin{equation}
          \label{eq:15}
          J_{\text{net}} = J_1 - J_{2} = \sigma_B(T_1^4 - T_2^4).
        \end{equation}
        \end{answer}

  \item A thin black plane---a heat shield---is placed between the plates, parallel to both.
  In the steady state, find the temperature \( T_s \) of the shield and the new net flux \( J_s \).
        \begin{answer}
          Since the shield is a black body, it absorbs all incident radiation, so that the net flux at the shield from the top plate is
          \begin{equation}
          \label{eq:16}
          J_{\text{top}} = J_1 - J_s = \sigma_B(T_1^4 - T_s^4),
        \end{equation}
        and the net flux at the bottom plate is
        \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:17}
        J_{\text{bottom}} = J_s - J_s = \sigma_B(T_s^4 - T_2^4).
      \end{equation}

      Now, for the system to be in equilibrium, the heat shield must be at constant temperature, i.e., \( J_{\text{top}} = J_{\text{bottom}} \).
      Solving for \( T_s \) gives
      \begin{equation}
      \label{eq:18}
      T_s^4 - T_2^4 = T_1^4 - T_s^4 \Rightarrow T_s^{4} = \frac{1}{2}(T_1^{4} + T_2^4).
      \end{equation}

      Finally, the net flux between the two plates is
      \begin{equation}
      \label{eq:19}
      \begin{aligned}
        J_s &= J_1 - J_s = \sigma_B(T_1^4 - T_s^4) = \sigma_B(T_1^4 - 1/2 T_1^4 - 1/2 T_2^{4}) \\
        &= \sigma_B(\frac{1}{2} T_1^4 - \frac{1}{2} T_2^{4}) = \frac{1}{2} J.
      \end{aligned}
      \end{equation}
        \end{answer}

  \item A black surface at room temperature (\( 300\,\mathrm{K} \)) faces a black surface at liquid-helium temperature (\( 4\,\mathrm{K} \)).
  \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item Find the net energy flux density \( J \) (power per unit area) from the room-temperature surface to the liquid helium.
          \begin{answer}
            From part (a), \( J = \sigma_B(300^4 - 4^4) = \pi^2k_B^4/60\hbar^3 c^3 (300^4 - 4^4) \approx 4.6 \times 10^2 \text{ W/m}^2\)
          \end{answer}
    \item A single heat shield is now placed between the room-temperature surface and the liquid helium.
    Find the equilibrium temperature of the shield and the new net energy flux density.
          \begin{answer}
            From part (b), the shield cuts the flux in half, so that \( J = 2.3 \times 10^2 \text{ W/m}^2 \) and the temperature of the shield is \( (1/2(300^4 + 4^4))^{1/4} \approx 252.27 \text{ K}\).
          \end{answer}
  \end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
